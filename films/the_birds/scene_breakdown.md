# The Birds: scene breakdown

Source: Aristophanes, *The Birds* (first staged 414 BC), anonymous English translation,
[Project Gutenberg eBook #3013](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/3013/3013-h/3013-h.htm) (public domain in the US).
Character names follow that translation (Pisthetaerus, Euelpides, Epops, Posidon).

## The story in brief

Two middle-aged Athenians, sick of a city where everyone is always suing everyone, walk out of Athens
to find Tereus, a former king who was turned into a hoopoe. They hope he knows of a quiet place to live.
Instead, one of them, Pisthetaerus, has a bigger idea: the birds should build a walled city in the sky,
between the gods and the humans. Humans send their offerings to the gods as smoke from sacrifices. A
city in the way can block that smoke, starve the gods, and make the birds rulers of the universe. The
birds attack the humans, are talked round, and build Cloudcuckooland (Nephelococcygia). A string of
chancers and hangers-on arrives to cash in and is chased off. The goddess Iris is caught trespassing
through the new city. Prometheus sneaks in to say the gods are starving. A delegation of gods is won
over by a barbecue. The play ends with Pisthetaerus marrying Basileia, a princess who keeps Zeus's
thunderbolt, and flying off as king of the gods.

The play runs about 16,000 words of dialogue, roughly 1,765 lines in the Greek and close to two hours
on stage. It follows the usual structure of Greek Old Comedy:

| Part | What it is | Scenes below |
|---|---|---|
| Prologue | Setup, the big idea | 1–3 |
| Parodos | The chorus enters | 4–6 |
| Agon | The formal debate | 7 |
| Parabasis | The chorus speaks to the audience | 8–9 |
| Episodes | The plan plays out, a series of visitors | 10–19 |
| Exodos | Finale and procession | 20 |

## Scene by scene

Each scene lists where it starts in the Gutenberg text, so the dialogue is easy to find.

### 1. Lost in the wilderness
- **Where:** a wild, rocky scrubland; broken rocks and brushwood, with a thicket in a cliff face.
- **Who:** Euelpides with a jay, Pisthetaerus with a crow.
- **Starts:** at the top of the play ("Do you think I should walk straight for yon tree?").
- **What happens:** the two men have walked in circles for miles behind two birds they bought from Philocrates the bird-seller, who promised the birds would lead them to Tereus. The birds only peck. Turning to the audience, they explain the trip: Athenians spend their whole lives in the law courts, so they have packed a basket, a pot and myrtle boughs to go and look for a quiet city. The crow and the jay start pointing up at the rock. The men knock by banging on it with a stone.
- **Look:** two tiny figures in a big, empty landscape. Comedy from the birds on their wrists.

### 2. The servant bird
- **Who:** Trochilus, Euelpides, Pisthetaerus.
- **Starts:** when Trochilus answers the knock ("Who's there? Who calls my master?").
- **What happens:** a bird with an enormous beak bursts out, sure they are bird-catchers. Terrified, they claim to be birds themselves. Euelpides calls himself "the Fearling, an African bird". Both let go of their guide birds, which fly away. Trochilus explains that he became a bird so he could keep serving his master, who has just fallen asleep after a meal of myrtle berries and gnats. He grudgingly goes to wake him.
- **Look:** a jump scare, then deflation.

### 3. The Hoopoe and the big idea
- **Who:** Epops, Euelpides, Pisthetaerus.
- **Starts:** when Epops orders the thicket opened ("Open the forest, that I may go out!").
- **What happens:**
  - Epops emerges, mostly bald from moulting, with a triple crest. He was once Tereus, king of Thrace.
  - He asks what kind of city they want. Euelpides wants one where the worst trouble is a friend insisting you come to his wedding feast. Epops suggests a few places, including a city on the Red Sea; they turn each one down.
  - Epops describes life as a bird: no purse to carry, and gardens of sesame, myrtle, poppies and mint.
  - **The idea:** Pisthetaerus has Epops look down, then up, then all around. The air between Earth and heaven is the birds' own territory. Found a city there and wall it in. Humans reach the gods through the smoke of their sacrifices, so the birds can charge the gods a toll on it, or starve them. Epops is thrilled and agrees to call a council of the birds.

### 4. The summoning
- **Who:** Epops, Procne (heard only, as a flute), Pisthetaerus, Euelpides.
- **Starts:** "Chase off drowsy sleep, dear companion" (the flute plays offstage).
- **What happens:** Epops wakes Procne the nightingale in the thicket. Then he sings the great summoning song, calling birds of the fields and orchards, the mountains, the marshes and the sea. For a long moment the men see nothing.
- **Look:** the most lyrical passage in the play. An empty sky, then the first bird.

### 5. The birds arrive
- **Who:** the Chorus of 24 birds, Epops, Pisthetaerus, Euelpides.
- **Starts:** "Torotina, torotina": the flamingo arrives.
- **What happens:** birds arrive one by one, each greeted with a joke. First a crimson flamingo, then "the Mede" (a Persian bird), a second crested hoopoe and the "glutton". Then a whole cloud of them: partridge, francolin, pochard, kingfisher, owl, magpie, turtle-dove, swallow, and blackbirds by the dozen. The chorus leader demands to know who called them.
- **Look:** spectacle. A catalogue of species, then a sky full of birds.

### 6. Ambush: the battle of the pots
- **Who:** the Chorus, Epops, Pisthetaerus, Euelpides.
- **Starts:** "Popopopopopopopoi. Where is he who called me?"
- **What happens:** Epops announces he has welcomed two humans. The birds, whose ancestral enemy is the bird-catcher, feel betrayed. They form ranks and charge, beaks first. The men hold them off with kitchenware from their luggage: a pot as a shield and skewers as spears. Epops talks the birds into lowering their weapons and letting the men speak.
- **Look:** an action set piece in a slapstick war-film style.

### 7. The pitch (the debate)
- **Who:** Pisthetaerus, the Chorus, Epops, Euelpides.
- **Starts:** "I am bursting with desire to speak; I have already mixed the dough of my speech."
- **What happens:**
  - Pisthetaerus proves the birds were the first kings, older than the Earth, older than the gods. The lark had to bury her father in her own head, because there was no ground yet. The cockerel was once king of Persia and still wears the crown. The kite ruled Greece, and the cuckoo ruled Egypt.
  - The birds are moved to tears by their lost glory.
  - The plan: wall in the air; demand the sceptre from Zeus; if he refuses, declare a holy war and bar the gods from crossing to visit their mortal lovers. Humans must sacrifice to the birds first. In return, the birds will eat the locusts, point humans to buried treasure, warn sailors of storms, and add centuries to their lives.
  - The birds are converted: the old man they hated is now their dearest friend.

### 8. Wings, and the nightingale
- **Who:** Epops, Pisthetaerus, Euelpides, the Chorus, Procne, the slaves.
- **Starts:** "Let us go in", and the chorus's request to see the nightingale.
- **What happens:** Epops invites the men into his nest. They will chew a certain root and grow wings. The birds beg Epops to bring out Procne. She appears, lovely and covered in gold, and the two men are smitten. Everyone goes inside.

### 9. The parabasis: the birds address the audience
- **Who:** the Chorus.
- **Starts:** "Weak mortals, chained to the earth, creatures of clay".
- **What happens:**
  - The birds' own creation story. In the beginning there was Chaos and Night. Night laid an egg, and out of it hatched golden-winged Eros. Eros mated with Chaos and produced the birds, before any god was born.
  - What birds do for humans: the crane flying south says it's time to sow, the kite's return says it's time to shear the sheep, and the swallow says it's time to trade winter clothes for light ones. Birds are also omens.
  - A sales pitch: with wings, a bored member of the audience could fly home in the middle of a tedious tragedy, eat lunch, and fly back.
- **Film note:** this is a natural voice-over for an opening title sequence.

### 10. Winged: naming Cloudcuckooland
- **Who:** Pisthetaerus, Euelpides (both now winged), Epops, the Chorus.
- **Starts:** when they come back out ("What makes you laugh?").
- **What happens:** the new wings look ridiculous. Pisthetaerus says Euelpides looks like a goose painted by a hack; Euelpides says Pisthetaerus looks like a close-shaven blackbird. They name the city Nephelococcygia, Cloudcuckooland, and choose a bird as its guardian. Pisthetaerus sends Euelpides up to supervise the wall builders, and he is never seen again. Heralds go to the gods and to humankind. Pisthetaerus prepares a founding sacrifice.

### 11. The intruders
- **Who:** Pisthetaerus, then six visitors in turn, with the Chorus.
- **Starts:** "Priest! 'tis high time! Sacrifice to the new gods."
- **What happens:** as soon as the city exists, the hustlers arrive, and each is beaten or chased off.
  - **The Priest** prays to a comically long list of bird-gods, until Pisthetaerus sends him away and finishes the sacrifice himself.
  - **The Poet** has already written odes to a city that is an hour old. He begs for a tunic, gets one, and leaves still singing.
  - **The Prophet** sells oracles that conveniently demand gifts for prophets. Pisthetaerus answers with a made-up oracle of his own that calls for a beating.
  - **Meton the geometer** arrives with rulers and compasses to square the circle and lay out the sky in city blocks. He is warned off, then chased.
  - **The Inspector**, an Athenian official chosen by lot, wants his salary. He gets beaten.
  - **The Dealer in Decrees** reads out Athenian laws for sale. He is beaten too, and he and the Inspector try to sue on their way out.
- **Film note:** ideal material for a fast montage.

### 12. The second parabasis
- **Who:** the Chorus.
- **Starts:** "Henceforth it is to me that mortals must address their sacrifices".
- **What happens:** the birds proclaim themselves gods, put a bounty on Philocrates the bird-seller, and sing about the easy life of birds. They finish by promising the judges good fortune if they award the play first prize, and bird droppings if they don't.
- **Film note:** full of topical Athenian jokes; cut.

### 13. The wall (messenger speech)
- **Who:** Pisthetaerus, the First Messenger, the Chorus.
- **Starts:** "Where, where is he? Where, where, where is he?"
- **What happens:** a messenger runs in to report that the wall is finished. It is wide enough for two chariots to pass, drawn by horses as big as the Trojan Horse. The work was done entirely by birds:
  - 30,000 cranes from Libya carried the foundation stones in their gullets.
  - Corncrakes chiselled the stones, and 10,000 storks made bricks.
  - Plovers and other water birds carried water up into the air.
  - Herons carried mortar in hods, with geese shovelling it in with their feet.
  - Ducks carried bricks, and swallows flew in with trowels on their backs.
  - **The pelicans were the carpenters, squaring off the gates with their beaks until it sounded like a shipyard.**
  - Sentries patrol with bells, and beacons burn on the towers.
- **Film note:** the play only describes this; the film can show it. It is our biggest visual set piece, and **our existing pelican asset has a real role in it.**

### 14. The breach: Iris
- **Who:** Pisthetaerus, the Second Messenger, Iris, the Chorus.
- **Starts:** "A horrible outrage has occurred": the second messenger.
- **What happens:** a winged god has slipped past the jays on day watch. Thirty thousand hawks take off after the intruder, joined by kestrels, buzzards, vultures and owls. Iris sweeps in, rainbow-winged. Pisthetaerus interrogates her like a border guard: did she get clearance from the storks? She threatens him with Zeus's thunderbolts. He threatens to send six hundred porphyrion birds up to heaven; a giant of the same name once gave Zeus a hard fight. She leaves in a fury.
- **Look:** an aerial chase, then a comic standoff.

### 15. News from Earth
- **Who:** Pisthetaerus, the Herald, the Chorus, Manes.
- **Starts:** "Oh! blessed Pisthetaerus, very wise, very illustrious".
- **What happens:** the herald brings a golden crown and news that humans are crazy about birds. People are taking bird nicknames, and more than 10,000 of them are coming to ask for wings. Pisthetaerus has baskets of wings brought out.

### 16. The wing-seekers
- **Who:** Pisthetaerus, a Parricide, Cinesias, an Informer.
- **Starts:** "Oh! might I but become an eagle": the Parricide arrives.
- **What happens:**
  - **The Parricide** wants wings because bird law lets a son beat his father. Pisthetaerus gives him black wings and spurs, tells him to imagine a cock's crest on his head, and sends him off to be a soldier in Thrace instead.
  - **Cinesias**, a poet of airy nonsense, wants wings for inspiration. He is chased off.
  - **The Informer**, who makes his living by bringing charges, wants wings to deliver court summonses to the islands faster. He is given "Corcyraean wings", which turn out to be whips, and is whipped away.

### 17. Choral interlude: strange things seen from the air
- **Starts:** "In my ethereal flights I have seen many things new and strange".
- **Film note:** topical satire; cut.

### 18. Prometheus under an umbrella
- **Who:** Pisthetaerus, Prometheus.
- **Starts:** "Ah! by the gods! if only Zeus does not espy me!"
- **What happens:** a masked figure sneaks in, asking nervously what the weather is doing and hiding under an umbrella so Zeus can't see him from above. It is Prometheus. He reports that since the city was built, no sacrifice smoke has reached Olympus, and the gods are starving. The barbarian gods are threatening to revolt, and envoys are on their way to make peace. His advice: accept no peace unless Zeus hands the sceptre back to the birds and gives Pisthetaerus Basileia as his wife. He leaves still hidden under the umbrella.

### 19. The embassy: the barbecue vote
- **Who:** Posidon, Heracles, Triballus, Pisthetaerus, a Servant.
- **Starts:** "This is the city of Nephelococcygia, Cloud-cuckoo-town".
- **What happens:**
  - Posidon is trying to make Triballus wear his cloak properly. Heracles wants to strangle whoever built the wall.
  - Pisthetaerus ignores them and cooks. He is roasting birds that were executed for rebelling against the people's friends.
  - Heracles is won over by the smell. Pisthetaerus demands the sceptre, then Basileia.
  - Posidon objects. Pisthetaerus takes Heracles aside: as Zeus's illegitimate son he would inherit nothing anyway.
  - The deciding vote goes to Triballus, whose broken gibberish is read as a yes ("Big bird give daughter pretty and queen"). Posidon is outvoted two to one.
  - Pisthetaerus goes up to heaven to collect his bride.
- **Look:** a comedy of manners, with the ridiculous vote as its payoff.

### 20. The wedding
- **Who:** a Messenger, the Chorus, Pisthetaerus, Basileia.
- **Starts:** "Oh, you, whose unbounded happiness I cannot express in words".
- **What happens:** a messenger announces Pisthetaerus's return, blazing like the sun. He holds Zeus's thunderbolt, has his bride at his side, and clouds of incense swirl around them. The chorus sings the wedding hymn and a hymn to the thunderbolt. Pisthetaerus takes Basileia by the hand, lifts her into the air, and the whole flock follows the couple in procession to Zeus's palace. He is hailed as the greatest of the gods.
- **Look:** the finale, a flight into the sky with a cast of thousands.

## Adapting it into a short film

A faithful adaptation would run close to two hours. The proposal below keeps the main storyline and
the big visual moments, and comes to about **13 minutes**. It turns reported action into shown action.
It cuts the direct addresses to the audience and the jokes about Athenian politicians of 414 BC, which
need footnotes today.

| # | Film scene | From play scenes | Target length | Why keep it |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| F1 | Title and creation story: Night's egg hatches Eros, then the birds (voice-over) | 9 | 0:45 | A mythic opening. Sets the tone and the "birds came first" idea |
| F2 | Lost in the rocks; the servant bird | 1–2 | 1:00 | The setup, plus a jump-scare laugh |
| F3 | The Hoopoe and the big idea | 3 | 1:30 | The premise |
| F4 | The summoning; the birds arrive | 4–5 | 1:30 | Spectacle: song, then a sky full of birds |
| F5 | The battle of the pots and the pitch | 6–7 | 1:30 | Action, then the persuasion |
| F6 | Wings, the nightingale, naming Cloudcuckooland | 8, 10 | 1:00 | The transformation |
| F7 | **Building the wall**, with pelican carpenters at the gates | 13 | 1:15 | The biggest set piece; shown, not told |
| F8 | The intruders (Meton, the Inspector, the Dealer in Decrees) | 11, 16 | 0:45 | Comic relief; one fast montage |
| F9 | Iris breaches the wall; the hawks give chase | 14 | 1:00 | Aerial action; the first sign of trouble with the gods |
| F10 | Prometheus under the umbrella | 18 | 0:45 | The twist: the gods are starving |
| F11 | The embassy and the barbecue vote | 19 | 1:30 | The comic climax |
| F12 | The wedding flight with the thunderbolt | 20 | 0:45 | The finale |

**Cut or folded in:** the second parabasis (12), the herald (15), most of the wing-seekers (16), the
choral interludes (17 and the others), the Priest, the Poet and the Prophet (11), and the dark Tereus
and Procne backstory.

**Choices to make before building:**
1. **Dialogue or not.** Old Comedy actors wore masks. Masked human characters would be authentic to
   the period and would avoid lip-sync animation altogether. Dialogue could then be voice-over, title
   cards, or no words at all, with music and sound telling the story.
2. **Euelpides's ending.** The play drops him after scene 10. Suggestion: he is the foreman on the wall
   in F7 and gets a moment at the wedding in F12.
3. **Tone of the ending.** Played straight, the finale is a triumph. The play can also be read as a
   satire of a con man who takes over heaven. The final shot can lean either way.
4. **Rating.** The play has bawdy jokes and a roast of "rebel" birds. Decide how family-friendly the
   film should be.

## What to build

**Environments**
- **E1, the rocky wilderness:** scrub, boulders, and a cliff with the Hoopoe's thicket door (F2–F5).
- **E2, Cloudcuckooland:** a field of clouds, the ring wall with gates, towers and beacons, and a
  rooftop kitchen and altar for the embassy (F6–F11).
- **E3, the sky finale:** the wedding procession and the gates of Zeus's palace (F12).

**Characters**
- **Humans in comic masks:** Pisthetaerus and Euelpides, in both wingless and winged versions; the
  intruders as costume variants of the same base.
- **Hero birds:** the Hoopoe, Trochilus, Procne.
- **The flock:** 15 to 20 species, with the chorus's 24 as hero birds and thousands for the wall and
  hawk sequences. **The pelican already exists**; it becomes the gate carpenter.
- **Gods:** Iris, Prometheus, Posidon, Heracles, Triballus, Basileia.

**Props**
- the jay and the crow
- a stone to knock with
- pots, skewers and plates
- the root, and baskets of wings
- sacrificial fire and smoke
- rulers and a compass
- the umbrella
- the trident, the lion skin and club
- a grill for the roast
- the sceptre and the thunderbolt

**New capabilities the pipeline needs**
1. **Human figures:** the first ones in this project. Masked, stylized characters keep this manageable.
2. **A modular bird generator:** body, beak, crest, wings and colours as parameters, reusing the loft
   and tube methods from the pelican, plus a shared wing-flap cycle.
3. **Crowds and flocks:** instanced birds with varied flapping, for the chorus, the wall builders and
   the hawks.
4. **Clouds:** volumetric clouds in Cycles look best but render slowly; stylized mesh clouds are a
   cheaper option.
5. **Sound:** music and a voice-over plan, to decide once the dialogue question is settled.

**Suggested order:**
1. Style frames for three key moments: the Hoopoe's first appearance, the wall under construction, and
   the wedding flight.
2. The bird generator and the flap cycle.
3. The masked human base.
4. The E1 and E2 blockouts.
5. A rough Workbench animatic of all twelve film scenes, before any final rendering.
